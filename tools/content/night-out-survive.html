A pub crawl should be an enjoyable part of your trip, not a test of how much you can drink. You can take part in conversations, music and a group night out without matching anyone else's orders or staying until the end.

This guide replaces the old drinking-challenge approach with practical planning: decide what suits you, keep control of your return journey and take a concern about another guest seriously. It is not medical advice or a promise that any activity is risk-free.

Before leaving your accommodation

Save your accommodation's actual address and check how to get back inside. A reception desk may close, an entry code may change or a key may need to stay with you. Ask the property about anything unclear before heading out, rather than discovering it at the door later.

Charge your phone, keep access to your own money and bring the essentials you need for the evening. Keep valuables under your control instead of leaving them in somebody else's bag. Choose a return arrangement that does not depend on meeting a new acquaintance or waiting for the entire group.

Choose your own pace

There is no need to get your money's worth by ordering as much as possible. An included drink, shot or open-bar period is not an instruction to consume it. Check what you actually want, ask about non-alcoholic options and feel free to decline a round.

Tell friends plainly if you are taking a break or leaving. Do not let a countdown, drinking game or comparison with another guest set your choices. If the evening no longer feels right, stopping is a reasonable decision, not something the group should turn into a joke.

Make a group plan that works

Agree how to contact one another and what to do if someone wants to go. A useful plan has clear choices, not one reluctant friend expected to manage everybody else's night. Check in before changing venue and avoid assuming that a person who has gone quiet is simply ready to be left alone.

Speak with the event leader or venue staff if you need help finding the group or dealing with a concern. Their presence does not replace emergency services, and you do not need their permission to call for urgent help. Make sure someone can describe where you are rather than relying only on a group-chat location pin.

Respect other people's choices

People can decline a drink, a game, a photograph, a conversation or intimate contact. Do not pressure them to explain, film them for entertainment or offer more alcohol to change their mind. A good shared evening leaves everyone free to choose differently.

If somebody wants to leave an interaction, give them room to do so. Do not share photos or private messages without permission. The nightlife dating guide covers considerate social plans and boundaries without promising a romantic outcome.

Keep your return journey independent

Check a route or transport option before you need it, then confirm current service details when leaving. Keep your accommodation address available offline. Do not assume that a daytime route, timetable or entrance will work in the early hours.

Arrange your own transport rather than depending on a stranger or following an unfamiliar group. If you are concerned about your ability to travel, speak with staff and a trusted person instead of setting off alone. Back at a shared property, keep noise down and follow its entry and visitor rules; the hostel etiquette guide covers considerate returns.

Know when to seek urgent help

If someone cannot be woken, is having a seizure or has slow, irregular or stopped breathing, call 112 in Poland now. If you suspect alcohol poisoning, do not wait for every possible symptom. Stay with the person and follow the dispatcher's instructions. Do not send them away alone or assume they can sleep it off.

The NHS alcohol-poisoning page explains these warning signs. Poland's Ministry confirms 112 is the free emergency number. Give your location and explain what has happened; this website cannot assess an emergency.

Do not rely on a quick fix

Coffee, a cold shower or a walk do not reverse an alcohol overdose. Do not use them as a reason to delay urgent help. NIAAA's overdose guidance explains why apparent sleep is not reassurance.

Do not turn a friend feeling unwell into a challenge, a photo opportunity or an argument about who can handle their drink. Take the concern seriously and make the immediate next step getting appropriate help, not preserving the plan for the evening.
